# Starting a startup with almost no money

A small budget is not the real problem. The real risk is spending weeks building something nobody pays
for. With ₱/$ very little, your best tools are **conversations, a demo, and follow-up**, and those are free.

## Step 1: start with a service, not a platform

Selling a **service** (you build a website for one restaurant) earns money in weeks and teaches you what
customers really need. Once the same requests repeat (every restaurant wants "edit my menu myself"), turn
that into a **product** (your menu CMS). Building the platform first means guessing.

Good first offers for restaurants, cheapest to deliver first:
1. **Google Business Profile** set-up and clean-up (hours, photos, menu, map pin): free for them to have; huge for being found.
2. **A one-page website** with the menu, hours, map, Messenger/phone button and a **QR code** for tables.
3. **Menu updates as a monthly care plan** (you update prices and photos when they message you).
4. Later: an admin page so they edit the menu themselves, then a simple FAQ chatbot, then ordering.

## Step 2: talk to customers before you build

- **Talk to 15-20 restaurant owners or managers** in two weeks. You're learning, not selling.
- Ask about their past and present, not your idea (from Rob Fitzpatrick's book *The Mom Test*):
  - "How do customers find you now?"
  - "When did you last change your menu or prices? How did customers find out?"
  - "Have you ever tried a website or Facebook ads? What happened?"
  - "What takes too much of your time each week?"
- Opinions ("nice idea!") are worthless; **actions** count: they give you their menu, agree to a demo, pay a deposit.
- Write down every answer. After 10 conversations you'll know what to offer.

## Why the AI calls got no answers

Automated or scripted calls fail with small businesses because:
- **Wrong time:** restaurants are busy before and during lunch and dinner. Visit or call mid-afternoon on a weekday.
- **Generic script:** it sounds like spam. Owners hang up in seconds.
- **No proof:** "do you want a website?" is abstract. A website of *their* restaurant on your phone is not.
- **No trust:** a stranger's voice (or a robot) asking for money. A face, a referral or a local connection works far better.
- **No follow-up:** most deals happen on the 2nd-5th contact, not the first.

## What to say instead

**Walk-in (best for restaurants; go at 2-4 pm):**
> "Hi, I'm [name], a student developer from [school/area]. I made a sample menu page for [restaurant name]:
> may I show you? It takes one minute." *(show your phone)* "Customers can see your menu and hours on
> Google and scan this QR at the table. I'm setting it up for a few local restaurants. What do you think
> would make it more useful for you?"

**Facebook Page message (many restaurants answer there):**
> "Hi [name]! I'm a local student developer. I made a free sample menu page for [restaurant]: [link].
> If you like it, I can put it on your Google listing and make a QR code for your tables. Would you like
> me to update anything (prices, photos)?"

**Follow-up after 3-4 days:**
> "Hi, just following up on the sample menu page for [restaurant]. I can make the first one free if I can
> use it as an example for other restaurants. Shall I set it up this week?"

**Common objections:**
| They say | You answer |
|---|---|
| "We already have Facebook." | "Perfect, I'll link it. The page shows up on Google search and Maps too, where people look for food nearby, and your menu is always up to date." |
| "We can't afford it." | "The first month is free so you can see if customers use it. After that it's [small monthly fee] for updates, cancel anytime." |
| "We're too busy." | "I'll do everything. I only need your menu photo and 5 minutes to check it." |
| "Maybe later." | "No problem. Can I check back next week? I'll leave the sample link here." |

## Your first offer and pricing

- **First 3 clients:** free or heavily discounted **in exchange for a testimonial, a referral and permission to show their site**.
- Then a **simple price**: a one-time setup fee plus an optional monthly care plan (menu updates, hosting, small changes).
- Price by value and local rates, not by your hours. Ask 2-3 local freelancers or agencies what they charge
  so your numbers are grounded (don't guess).
- Put it in writing: what's included, how many updates per month, payment terms.

## What to do with a very small budget (like $23)

| Spend on | Why |
|---|---|
| Nothing for hosting at first | Demos and simple sites host free (GitHub Pages, Netlify, Cloudflare Pages). |
| Printing a few QR "table tent" samples | Physical proof wins conversations. |
| Transport and snacks for visits | Conversations are your real work this month. |
| A domain only when a client pays | Clients usually pay for their own domain. |

Don't spend on ads or paid tools until a restaurant has paid you.

## The first 30 days

| Week | Goal (measurable) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Make 3 sample menu pages (real restaurants nearby, from their public photos); talk to 10 owners |
| 2 | Talk to 10 more; get 3 "yes, set it up" |
| 3 | Deliver 3 free/discounted sites; collect testimonials; ask each for one referral |
| 4 | Offer the paid version to 10 new restaurants; goal: 1-2 paying clients |

**Stop/continue rule:** if after 30 conversations nobody wants even the free version, change the offer
(for example Google Business Profile set-up only, or social media menu posts) or the customer type
(cafés, salons, clinics). Decide by the numbers, not your feelings that day.

## Before you make money (Philippines)

When you start earning regularly, register properly: a business name (DTI for sole proprietors), the BIR,
and your city or municipality's business permit. Requirements and fees change; check the official sites or
ask your local business office. Keep simple records of every payment from the start.

## From service to startup

Keep a list of every request clients make. When 3+ clients ask for the same thing (edit menu myself,
online orders, reservations), that's your product. Build it once (a multi-restaurant CMS), move your
existing clients onto it, and now you have a SaaS with recurring revenue and real users.
